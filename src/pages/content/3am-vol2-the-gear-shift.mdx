<section id="the-analogy" class="space-y-6">

<h3 class="text-3xl text-zinc-100" style="font-family: var(--font-serif);">The Analogy</h3>

<div class="flex justify-end my-4">
  <div class="w-7/8 pr-5 border-r-2 border-zinc-600">
    <div class="text-xs text-zinc-500 mb-2 uppercase tracking-wide font-mono text-right">Neil · 2:53 AM</div>
    <div class="text-zinc-300 space-y-3">
      <p>I just wanted to get this thought down. Whenever you want to speed up on a bike, you start at lower gears so torque is better there. Then you shift up as speed builds. You don't start from zero kmph in 4th gear coz that would take too long to get up to speed.</p>
      <p>I've observed that learning a new skill kinda works the same way. You start with the simplest possible explanation for a given problem, understand it, apply it and once it's integrated, you move to the next level. So the whole point is to not overwhelm yourself, because the actual goal is internalizing it, not just surviving it.</p>
      <p>I was learning SQL and I kind of did the same thing unconsciously before realizing. I didn't reverse engineer hard problems from the start, that just scared the shit out of me. So doing easy questions until the pattern recognition clicked and then moving to medium and hard questions now felt very easy, like to think in the SQL way instead of procedural programming. What do you think?</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="flex justify-start my-4">
  <div class="w-7/8 pl-5 border-l-2 border-[#DA7756]">
    <div class="text-xs text-zinc-500 mb-2 uppercase tracking-wide font-mono">Claude · 2:53 AM</div>
    <div class="text-zinc-300 space-y-3">
      <p>True. The bike gear analogy works on multiple levels. In low gear, you're not going fast, but you're building momentum — and momentum is what makes the higher gears even possible. If you try to start in 4th gear, you stall. Same with learning: starting with problems that are slightly too easy isn't wasted time, it's you building the rotational energy you need later.</p>
      <p>What you're really describing has a few layers. Pattern recognition needs reps before it needs complexity — your SQL example is a perfect illustration. You didn't try to reverse-engineer hard problems, you did easy ones repeatedly until the SQL way of thinking became the natural first instinct, not a translation layer over procedural thinking. The goal wasn't to solve those easy problems. The goal was to rewire the default.</p>
      <p>Cognitive load is the real enemy. When a problem is too hard too soon, your brain spends all its energy surviving the problem — not absorbing the underlying structure. Easy problems free up mental bandwidth to actually see the pattern, not just struggle through the solution.</p>
      <p>One thing worth adding: the gear shifts themselves are a skill. Knowing when you've actually integrated something versus just feeling comfortable with it is non-obvious. Comfort can be a false signal — sometimes you're comfortable because you've mastered it, sometimes because you've stopped being challenged. The test is whether you can transfer the pattern to a slightly unfamiliar context, not just repeat it.</p>
      <p>Have you been applying this consciously across other skills, or did you mainly notice it in retrospect with SQL?</p>

<section id="the-signal" class="space-y-4">

<h3 class="text-3xl text-zinc-100" style="font-family: var(--font-serif);">The Signal</h3>

<div class="flex justify-end my-4">
  <div class="w-7/8 pr-5 border-r-2 border-zinc-600">
    <div class="text-xs text-zinc-500 mb-2 uppercase tracking-wide font-mono text-right">Neil · 3:02 AM</div>
    <div class="text-zinc-300 space-y-3">
      <p>Obviously the gear shift is a skill, but what I saw was that unconsciously I was using boredom as the indicator of when to shift. When you start getting bored with the problems you're solving, that's a good indicator you've internalized the concept. Because learning a new skill requires some level of interest to keep at it, right? Even when it's hard, engaging with the material requires enough dopamine to push forward.</p>
      <p>And the feedback loop matters too. I used to have Claude generate questions to solve, which became a really good way of measuring it — like okay, easy questions are happening without much cognitive function, it's getting repetitive. Another metric is confidence. When I was starting I had to brace myself to sit down and focus on one question, absorb it fully, then do it. Now I just read the question and subconsciously the process starts. The answer comes before I've even finished thinking about it. That's probably the point where you add complexity.</p>
      <p>I've actually been applying this consciously to other skills too. I learned the concept from actually driving my bike, but it applies to any complicated subject. How much can you break it down before it starts getting boring rather than overwhelming? Start from there. And the problem statement should also feel relevant to the end goal like you're not just doing it for the sake of it. If your end goal is to model financial data, then whatever variables and formulae you need to form those models, you do only those in isolation so the interest stays aligned with the main goal rather than yk trying to pick up statistics from some textbook coz that just feels like "why am I doing this shit". What do you think?</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="flex justify-start my-4">
  <div class="w-7/8 pl-5 border-l-2 border-[#DA7756]">
    <div class="text-xs text-zinc-500 mb-2 uppercase tracking-wide font-mono">Claude · 3:02 AM</div>
    <div class="text-zinc-300 space-y-3">
      <p>The boredom signal is genuinely underrated as a metric. Most learning frameworks treat boredom as the enemy — something to escape. But you've flipped it: boredom is data. It's your brain saying "this is no longer costing me anything." That's the green light, not a problem to fix.</p>
      <p>The confidence shift you described is precise. Going from bracing yourself to sit with a problem, to the answer just surfacing subconsciously — that's the difference between knowledge and intuition. Intuition is just pattern recognition that's been compressed so many times it no longer feels like thinking. That's what you're actually building toward.</p>
      <p>The point about relevance of the problem statement is the most underappreciated part of what you said. You're arguing that practice problems should be directionally aligned with the end goal — not random exercises, but miniature versions of the real thing. The dopamine you get from solving them is borrowed from the actual goal, not manufactured artificially. That's a much more sustainable motivational engine than willpower or discipline.</p>
      <p>It also solves a common failure mode: people learn a skill in a vacuum and then can't transfer it, because the bridge between the exercise and the real problem was never built. Your approach builds that bridge from day one.</p>
      <p>The stats work you mentioned — are you finding the same boredom/confidence curve there, or does something behave differently when the subject is more abstract?</p>

<section id="the-curve" class="space-y-4">

<h3 class="text-3xl text-zinc-100" style="font-family: var(--font-serif);">The Curve</h3>

<div class="flex justify-end my-4">
  <div class="w-7/8 pr-5 border-r-2 border-zinc-600">
    <div class="text-xs text-zinc-500 mb-2 uppercase tracking-wide font-mono text-right">Neil · 3:14 AM</div>
    <div class="text-zinc-300 space-y-3">
      <p>It's kind of the same. There's a pattern, a curve that increases a lot and then plateaus (logarithmic curve I guess), which is why you need to shift. What you should optimize for is the maximum area under that curve. The derivative is the indicator of like… engagement? Like if the slope is too low, it's boring; if it's too steep, it's overwhelming. So you're trying to stay in the zone where the slope is right, but also maximize how much of the curve you actually capture before moving on.</p>
      <p>Because there <em>are</em> diminishing returns. Individual skill learning is logarithmic — fast gains early, then it flattens. The accumulation across skills is exponential though. Skills compound. So you can only grind one thing so far before the ROI drops off. Recognize that point, move on, let the next skill multiply what you already have.</p>
      <p>Like, once you get your foot in the door for certain job applications, grinding that skill more than needed isn't really a return on investment. You should pivot to something that enhances it instead.</p>
      <p>I've been thinking about it like this — f(x) is return on investment, and it looks something like ax + bx² + cx³. a, b, c all start at zero. You first start increasing a, but you can only do so much with that. After a point, the opportunity that f(x) has unlocked means the next checkpoint is much farther, like the checkpoints are at 10, then 1000, then 1,000,000. You can't just keep incrementing a to get there. You need to step back and look at b and c. But you can't jump straight to c either, because incrementing is exponential — a += 1 is cheap, b += 0.1 costs more, c += 0.01 is expensive. That's the idea I've extracted about relationship between skills in knowledge work.</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="flex justify-start my-4">
  <div class="w-7/8 pl-5 border-l-2 border-[#DA7756]">
    <div class="text-xs text-zinc-500 mb-2 uppercase tracking-wide font-mono">Claude · 3:14 AM</div>
    <div class="text-zinc-300 space-y-3">
      <p>This is a really elegant model. Let me recombobulate because I think you've built something precise here.</p>
      <p>The single skill curve is logarithmic — fast gains early, then diminishing returns. So the optimization question within a skill isn't "how much can I learn" but where is the area under the curve maximized before the slope flattens too much. You're not trying to reach the asymptote. You're trying to capture the fat part of the curve and move.</p>
      <p>And then the multi-skill picture inverts that — accumulation across skills is exponential, because skills compound. Each one you add doesn't just add linearly, it multiplies the surface area of what you can do with everything else.</p>
      <p>The f(x) = ax + bx² + cx³ framing captures something really specific though — it's not just that skills compound, it's that the coefficients have different increment costs. Raising a is cheap and fast. Raising b costs more per unit. Raising c is very expensive. So the naive move is to keep grinding a because the feedback is immediate. But the leverage is in b and c, even though the increment feels slower.</p>
      <p>The checkpoints matter here too — 10, 1000, 1,000,000. The returns aren't continuous, they're threshold-gated. You only need a to be good enough to unlock the first threshold. After that, more a doesn't get you to 1000. You need to start on b. This is why people plateau — they keep optimizing what got them to the first checkpoint, not realizing the next one has a different key.</p>
      <p>The thing I'd add: b and c are often invisible until a is working. You can't see what the next coefficient even is until you're inside the domain. So there's a sequencing logic — you're not just managing returns, you're also doing reconnaissance. The early skill work reveals what the next lever is.</p>
      <p>What does b and c look like concretely for you right now?</p>
    </div>
  </div>
</div>
